If you are looking for the **child support percentage in Oaxaca**, the short answer is: the law does not set one fixed percentage. The Family Court applies the **proportionality principle** —what the recipient needs and what the payer can afford— and the amount follows from that. The tables circulating online are guidelines, not an automatic rule.

In practice, one child with proven income and ordinary school expenses usually falls in the low double digits; three children with medical costs and a mortgage approach the cap. What defines the number is the evidence, not a calculator.

## Is there an official percentage table in Oaxaca?

No. The Family Code of the State of Oaxaca establishes the support obligation and its components —food, clothing, housing, health and education— but it does not publish a percentage table by number of children. The judge assesses each file.

That is why “the law says exactly 20%” should be treated with suspicion. The figure may match the outcome of one case, but it is not a rule. Anyone who quotes a percentage without reviewing income, expenses and evidence is guessing.

## Guideline ranges by number of children

These ranges reflect how cases with formal income and average needs are usually resolved in Oaxaca. **They are guidelines**: the judge can depart from them when the evidence justifies it.

| Children | Guideline % of net income | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | 15% – 25% | Moves toward 25% with medical costs or private school |
| 2 | 25% – 35% | Shared between both children, not doubled |
| 3+ | 40% – 50% | Common cap to protect the payer's minimum subsistence |

The percentage is not multiplied per child. With two children you do not claim 40% “because there are two”; the court sets one global amount that covers both and distributes it internally.

## What raises or lowers the amount

The judge weighs, among other factors:

- **The recipient's needs:** age, schooling, health, disability, housing and documented extraordinary expenses.
- **The payer's ability:** net income, other support obligations, proven debts and minimum subsistence.
- **The standard of living during the relationship:** if the family paid private school, that change cannot be ignored without explanation.
- **Unverifiable income:** informal work, own business, property, vehicles and bank movements.
- **Shared time:** with joint custody, support is adjusted to the days and expenses each parent assumes.

A common mistake is filing only a utility bill and a birth certificate. Moving the percentage requires an expense file: school fees, medical receipts, rent, transport and proof of the payer's income.

## Payers without verifiable income

If the obligor is self-employed, paid in cash or claims no income, the obligation does not disappear. The court can:

- Set an amount based on the **UMA** or the current minimum wage.
- Infer income from the standard of living: home, vehicles, cards, travel, business activity.
- Order socioeconomic studies and request information from institutions.

In these cases the investigation before filing matters as much as the litigation. A file with clear indicia avoids a symbolic amount that later has to be modified.

## Provisional support: the amount that matters first

When the claim is admitted, the judge can set **provisional support** while the case is decided. It is the money that reaches the family in the first weeks, and it is usually calculated with the same proportionality criteria, although with less evidence.

If provisional support is too low, it can be challenged with additional documentation. If the payer does not pay, enforcement is requested: wage withholding, attachment of accounts or assets, and registration in the child-support debtors registry.

## How to request an increase or reduction

Circumstances change. A promotion, new school fees, an illness or job loss justify reviewing the amount through an incidental proceeding. The request requires documentary proof of the change and can be resolved in a few months.

For an increase, prove: a real rise in the payer's income, new needs of the child, or a documented increase in existing ones. For a reduction: proven lower income, new family obligations or a change of custody.

## Mistakes that lower support

1. **Not documenting expenses.** Without receipts, the judge decides with what is on file.
2. **Asking for a “table” percentage.** The case is argued, not the table.
3. **Hiding your own income.** Proportionality runs both ways.
4. **Not challenging provisional support.** It can be reviewed and supplemented.
5. **Waiting years to request an increase.** Retroactivity has limits.

## Frequently asked questions

### What percentage of child support is paid in Oaxaca?
There is no single percentage in the law. The judge applies proportionality between the recipient's need and the payer's ability. As a guideline, 15% to 25% of net income is used for one child, 25% to 35% for two and 40% to 50% for three or more, with caps that protect the payer's minimum subsistence.

### Is the percentage based on gross or net income?
On proven net income, after mandatory deductions. When income is variable, the court can set an amount in UMA or minimum wages and adjust it using socioeconomic studies and documentary evidence.

### Can child support exceed 50% of income?
Only exceptionally. The judge must protect the payer's minimum subsistence and proportionality among all dependants. It can approach that limit when documented needs justify it.

### What if the other parent has no payroll income?
A lack of payroll does not extinguish the obligation. Income is inferred from bank statements, property, vehicles, business activity and witness evidence, and the court sets support based on those indicia and the UMA.

### Can I request an increase if school fees go up?
Yes. Changed circumstances can be raised through an incidental proceeding: schooling, illness, new needs of the child or a real increase in the payer's income justify reviewing the amount. Documentary proof of the change is required.
